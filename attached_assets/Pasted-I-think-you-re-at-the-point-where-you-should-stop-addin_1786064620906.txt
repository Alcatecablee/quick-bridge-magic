I think you're at the point where **you should stop adding sections and start increasing perceived quality.**

Reading your landing page, something stood out immediately.

QuickBridge no longer has a messaging problem.

It has a **presentation problem.**

The copy now tells a compelling story:

> Scan → Trust → One-click device cooperation

That's actually a very strong product narrative.

The page just **looks like a well-designed SaaS marketing site**, while the product itself is starting to feel like an operating-system feature.

Those two don't match anymore.

# Motion

I'd avoid "startup animation syndrome."

Don't animate everything.

Animate hierarchy.

For example:

Hero:

* Headline clip reveal
* Subtitle fade
* CTA rises
* Done

Feature story:

* Sticky section
* Phone gently floats
* Arrow draws
* Device glows
* Done

Comparison:

* Old workflow builds step-by-step
* QuickBridge appears instantly

Security:

* Thin lines draw
* Cards fade upward

Nothing spins.

Nothing bounces.

Nothing screams for attention.

---

# Visual style

I would lean heavily toward:

* Black background (#090909 instead of pure black)
* Very subtle grain texture
* Hairline borders
* Glass only where interaction matters
* Large typography (72-120px depending on viewport)
* Plenty of negative space
* Slow, confident motion
* Very restrained blue as the only accent color

The feeling should be closer to **Linear**, **Raycast**, **Apple**, and **OpenAI** than to a typical SaaS landing page.

# One thing I'd add

Right now the page tells users what Continuity *is*.

Show them what it *feels like*.

Imagine a full-width sticky sequence:

```
Laptop

reddit.com

↓

Click

↓

"Continue on Phone"

↓

Phone wakes

↓

Same page

↓

Same position

↓

Done.
```

No words.

Just captions.

The best landing pages in 2026 increasingly explain products through behavior instead of paragraphs. If someone understands QuickBridge after watching six seconds of interaction, you've achieved something far more valuable than another polished feature list.

If I were rebuilding QuickBridge today, I'd target **Apple's narrative**, **Linear's restraint**, **Raycast's clarity**, and **Arc Browser's sense of delight**, while avoiding the temptation to add flashy effects simply because WebGL exists. The product has matured enough that the interface should project confidence, not try to prove it's modern through constant motion.
